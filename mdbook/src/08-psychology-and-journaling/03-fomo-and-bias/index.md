# FOMO, overconfidence, confirmation bias

## Hook

Your plan says buy at Rs 400, with a stop at Rs 390. The price never comes back to Rs 400. Instead it jumps 12% in one morning, to Rs 448. You feel you must buy now, before it goes higher. That feeling has a name: fear of missing out, or FOMO. This chapter shows how FOMO changes a planned trade into a different trade. It also covers two quieter habits that bend your thinking, overconfidence and confirmation bias.

## The idea

**FOMO** is buying after a big move, because others seem to be buying. The planned price is gone. The trade you take is a new trade, with a new entry and a new stop.

A **late entry** needs its own numbers. Its stop is usually far from the plan. With the same Rs 500 of risk, a wider stop buys fewer shares.

**Overconfidence** means you read a few wins as proof of skill. A short run of wins is often luck. Treat a short run as a small sample, not a trend.

**Confirmation bias** means you look only for evidence that agrees with your idea. You skip the news and signs that disagree.

The counter-habit is simple. Before you enter, write at least one reason the trade could fail. Then test the idea against that reason, not only against the reasons you like.

## Worked example

**Hypothetical.** Planned entry Rs 400, stop Rs 390. Risk per share Rs 10. Risk budget Rs 500. This example looks only at the risk limit. It leaves out the cash limit to keep the sums short.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Planned shares | 500 / 10 | 50 shares |
| 2. Planned cash | 50 x 400 | Rs 20,000 |
| 3. Price after the 12% day | 400 x 1.12 | Rs 448 |
| 4. New stop | given | Rs 420 |
| 5. New risk per share | 448 - 420 | Rs 28 |
| 6. Late-entry shares | floor(500 / 28) = floor(17.86) | 17 shares |
| 7. Late-entry cash | 17 x 448 | Rs 7,616 |

The late entry buys fewer shares for the same Rs 500 of risk. It also uses less cash. But the entry price, the stop and the reason for the trade are all different from the plan.

The trade is no longer the planned trade. Fewer shares do not make it the planned trade.

## Try it

### Guided exercise

**Task.** Use the same figures as the worked example. Your late entry is at Rs 448. This time the new stop is Rs 435. Work out the risk per share, the shares, and the cash. Then say whether the trade is still the planned one.

Hints:

1. Risk per share = price now minus the new stop.
2. Shares = 500 divided by the risk per share, rounded down.
3. The planned trade had an entry of Rs 400 and a stop of Rs 390.

**Answer:**

- Risk per share: 448 - 435 = Rs 13.
- Shares: 500 / 13 = 38.46, rounded down to **38 shares**. Check: 13 x 38 = 494, which is within Rs 500. 13 x 39 = 507, which is over.
- Cash: 38 x 448 = **Rs 17,024**.
- The trade is **not** the planned one. The entry, the stop and the cash all differ from the plan.

### Independent exercise

**Task.** Write three reasons the trade idea you hold could fail. Then write one rule that would stop you from entering.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

There is no single right list. A good answer has three reasons that could really happen, written before you test the idea. Examples of the kind of reason to write:

- The move may already have used up most of its expected gain.
- The news you read may be old, or it may only support one side.
- The price may not be near your planned entry any more.

A rule might read: "I do not enter if the price is above my planned entry." Each rule must be a yes or no test.

All figures in this chapter are hypothetical.

</details>

## Paper task

Mode: **learning note**. Log it in your journal (or on paper until the journal app is ready).

1. Pick three trade ideas from your reading. Use learning notes only. Do not place any order.
2. For each idea, write the planned entry price and the planned stop first.
3. Write one reason the idea could fail. Do this before you test the idea.
4. If you imagine a late entry, write the new stop and the new shares. Mark the entry "not the planned trade" if the plan price has gone.
5. If the journal is not ready, do the task on paper or in a spreadsheet.

## Limits and common mistakes

- A disconfirming reason helps you think more clearly. It does not tell you what the price will do.
- A late entry is a new trade. It needs its own entry, stop and checks before you act on it.
- A short run of wins does not prove skill. A short run of losses does not prove the method is broken either.
- Knowing these habits does not remove them. Write the counter-habit down each time, so it is there when you need it.
- The numbers in this chapter are made up. They show the arithmetic, not a real stock.

## Remember

- FOMO is buying after a big move, when the planned price is gone.
- A late entry usually has a wider stop, so fewer shares fit the same rupee risk.
- Five wins in a row is a small sample. It is not proof.
- Confirmation bias is looking only for evidence that agrees with you.
- Before you enter, write one reason the trade could fail.
