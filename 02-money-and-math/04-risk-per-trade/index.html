Capital-First Trading

Risk per trade and stop-loss distance

Hook

Imagine you have Rs 50,000. You decide in advance to risk Rs 500 on a trade. Two plans both risk Rs 500 in total. One buys 50 shares. The other buys 25. The difference is not the budget. It is where each plan puts its stop, the price at which the idea is wrong. A stop far from the entry allows fewer shares for the same rupee risk. This chapter shows the arithmetic, step by step.

The idea

Start with the rule that comes first. Set the risk per trade before you make the trade. Turn it into a rupee amount based on your capital.

  • Capital is the money you have set aside for trading (The capital-first rules).
  • Risk percentage is the share of that capital you accept losing on one trade. A common planning figure is 1%. In this book it is a planning setting, not a fact.
  • Risk budget is capital x risk percentage, in rupees.

The stop is the price at which the trade idea is wrong. You pick it because of the idea, not to fit a budget. This order matters. If you pick the stop to fit the budget, the stop no longer says anything about the trade.

Once the stop is chosen, the rest is arithmetic. For a long trade (you buy first and sell later). This track covers long trades only:

  • Risk per share = entry price - stop price. This is the rupee loss on each share if the stop is hit.
  • Shares = risk budget / risk per share, rounded down to a whole number.
  • Stop distance as a percentage of entry = risk per share / entry price.
  • Position value = shares x entry price.
  • Planned loss at the stop = shares x risk per share.

Rounding down matters. If the formula gives 31.25 shares, you buy 31, not 32. Rounding up would risk more than the budget.

Two rules follow from the arithmetic:

  • A tighter stop (closer to entry) gives a smaller risk per share. So the same budget buys more shares.
  • A wider stop (further from entry) gives a larger risk per share. So the same budget buys fewer shares.

Two numbers are easy to confuse:

  • Risk percentage (1% of capital) is the planned loss as a share of capital.
  • Position percentage is the position value as a share of capital.

A 1% risk does not mean a 1% position. In the example below, a 1% risk gives a position of 25% of capital.

Rules for the stop itself:

  • A stop above the entry price is invalid for a long trade. The stop must sit below the entry. If the plan has a stop above the entry, reject the plan or fix it before you do any arithmetic.
  • Never move a stop further away after entry to avoid a loss. That turns a planned loss into an unplanned one.
  • Ways to place stops, based on how much a price usually moves, are taught in Track 5. This chapter covers only the arithmetic.

See it

Move the stop closer to the entry and watch the share count rise. Move it further away and watch the share count fall. The rupee risk stays near Rs 500 in each case. Look at the position value too. It changes with the share count, not with the risk budget.

Worked example

Hypothetical. Capital Rs 50,000. Risk 1% = Rs 500. Entry Rs 250.

Plan 1: stop at Rs 240

Plan 2: stop at Rs 245 (tighter)

StepCalculationResult

A 50% position is above the default 25% limit for one position. This limit is used in Lab 1. With the limit, the largest allowed position is 12,500 / 250 = 50 shares. The planned loss becomes 50 x 5 = Rs 250, not Rs 500. Here the limit, not the risk budget, sets the size. Position sizing step by step works through this in full.

Plan 3: stop at Rs 230 (wider)

StepCalculationResult
Risk per share250 - 230Rs 20

Position value25 x 250Rs 6,250
Position as % of capital6,250 / 50,00012.5%

Lesson. Plans 1 and 3 each plan a Rs 500 loss, with 50 and 25 shares. Plan 2 shows that a tighter stop does not always mean a bigger planned loss. The limit cut its size to 50 shares and the planned loss to Rs 250. The stop distance sets the share count before limits are applied. The limits and the risk budget then decide the final size.

Try it

Guided exercise

Task. Capital Rs 50,000, risk 1%, entry Rs 400, stop Rs 384. Work out the risk budget, the risk per share, the shares (rounded down), the position value, the position as a percentage of capital and the planned loss.

Hints:

  1. Find the risk budget first. It is 1% of Rs 50,000.
  2. Find the raw share count. Then round down before you do anything else with it.
  3. Work out the planned loss from the rounded share count, not the raw one.

Answer:

  • Risk budget: Rs 500.
  • Risk per share: 400 - 384 = Rs 16.
  • Raw shares: 500 / 16 = 31.25, so 31 shares.
  • Position value: 31 x 400 = Rs 12,400.
  • Position as % of capital: 12,400 / 50,000 = 24.8%.
  • Planned loss: 31 x 16 = Rs 496. The unused part of the budget is Rs 4.

Independent exercise

Task. Capital Rs 30,000, risk 0.5%, entry Rs 120, stop Rs 111. Work out the risk budget, the risk per share, the shares, the position value, the position percentage and the planned loss.

Check your answer
  • Risk budget: 30,000 x 0.5% = Rs 150.
  • Risk per share: 120 - 111 = Rs 9.
  • Raw shares: 150 / 9 = 16.67, so 16 shares.
  • Position value: 16 x 120 = Rs 1,920.
  • Position as % of capital: 1,920 / 30,000 = 6.4%.
  • Planned loss: 16 x 9 = Rs 144.

All figures are hypothetical.

Limits and common mistakes

  • It does not tell you where a good stop is. The stop must come from the reason for the trade. This chapter only does the arithmetic.
  • It does not show that a 1% planned risk is a 1% maximum loss. A gap (a jump in price with no trading in between) or a circuit (a trading halt set by the exchange) can make the loss bigger than planned. See Slippage, gaps and planned versus actual risk.
  • It does not make a tight stop safer. A very tight stop can be hit by ordinary small price moves. Then you can take many small losses.
  • It does not change the rule. Never raise the risk percentage after losses.

Remember

  • Set the risk budget first. It is capital x risk percentage, in rupees.
  • For a long trade, risk per share = entry - stop. A stop at or above the entry is invalid.
  • Shares = risk budget / risk per share, rounded down.
  • A tighter stop allows more shares for the same rupee risk. A wider stop allows fewer.
  • Risk percentage is not position percentage. 1% risk gave a 25% position in the example.
